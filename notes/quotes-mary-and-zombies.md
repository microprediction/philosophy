## Jackson's original (Epiphenomenal Qualia, Philosophical Quarterly 32(127), 1982, p. 130 by standard citation)
https://www.sfu.ca/~jillmc/JacksonfromJStore.pdf

"Mary is a brilliant scientist who is, for whatever reason, forced to investigate the world from a black and white room via a black and white television monitor. She specialises in the neurophysiology of vision and acquires, let us suppose, all the physical information there is to obtain about what goes on when we see ripe tomatoes, or the sky, and use terms like 'red', 'blue', and so on. She discovers, for example, just which wave-length combinations from the sky stimulate the retina, and exactly how this produces via the central nervous system the contraction of the vocal chords and expulsion of air from the lungs that results in the uttering of the sentence 'The sky is blue'. (It can hardly be denied that it is in principle possible to obtain all this physical information from black and white television, otherwise the Open University would of necessity need to use colour television.)
What will happen when Mary is released from her black and white room or is given a colour television monitor? Will she learn anything or not? It seems just obvious that she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it. But then it is inescapable that her previous knowledge was incomplete. But she had all the physical information. Ergo there is more to have than that, and Physicalism is false."

Same paper: "I am what is sometimes known as a 'qualia freak'." And, against himself: it is hard to give qualia a causal role "without sounding like someone who believes in fairies."

## Jackson's recantation

"Mind and Illusion" (2003), opening page. https://www.informationphilosopher.com/solutions/philosophers/jackson/Mind_And_Illusion.pdf
"Most contemporary philosophers given a choice between going with science and going with intuitions, go with science. Although I once dissented from the majority, I have capitulated and now see the interesting issue as being where the arguments from the intuitions against physicalism—the arguments that seem so compelling—go wrong."

Interview with James Garvey, The Philosophers' Magazine. https://philosophersmag.com/frank-jackson-latter-day-physicalist/
"In 'Epiphenomenal Qualia' I explain why it's not such a disaster being an epiphenomenalist, but I came to think of this as a triumph of philosophical ingenuity over common sense. This is what someone who's done a good philosophy degree can somehow make seem all right, but if you look at it in a more commonsensical way it's actually pretty implausible."
"So the epiphenomenal stuff was just very hard to believe."
On how the paper began: the Monash psychology department "sort of said we understand that you're one of the few dualists left on the planet. Would you like to give a talk saying why?"

## Rebukes of Mary's Room

Dennett, "What RoboMary Knows" (2007). https://web.archive.org/web/20130107011744/http://ase.tufts.edu/cogstud/papers/RoboMaryfinal.htm
"TRAD: Look. It's just obvious! You can't deduce what a color looks like if you've never seen one!
DCD: That's an interesting folk theorem, I must say. Here's another: If you burp, sneeze and fart all at the same time, you die. Both sound sort of plausible to me, but is there any scientific backing for either one of them?"

Dennett, Consciousness Explained (1991), pp. 399-400, as reprinted by Dennett in RoboMary (blue banana):
"Mary took one look at it and said 'Hey! You tried to trick me! Bananas are yellow, but this one is blue!' Her captors were dumfounded."
"Of course it's hard for you to imagine. It's hard for anyone to imagine the consequences of someone knowing absolutely everything physical about anything!"
p. 398: "In fact it is a bad thought experiment, an intuition pump that actually encourages us to misunderstand its premises!"

Dennett, RoboMary: "I had no idea philosophers still put so much faith in the authority of their homegrown intuitions. It is almost as if one thought one could prove that the Copernican theory was false by noting that it 'seems just obvious' that the earth doesn't move and the sun does."
"Wherever Block says 'Surely', look for what we might call a mental block."

David Lewis, "What Experience Teaches" (1988). https://beisecker.faculty.unlv.edu/Courses/PHIL-352/Dave%20-%20Consciousness%20PDFs/Lewis-WhatExperienceTeaches.PDF
"She may also do other things she didn't do before: unfortunate things, like writing about phenomenal information and the poverty of materialism."
"Lessons on the aura of Vegemite will do no more for us than lessons on its chemical composition."

Patricia Churchland, Neurophilosophy (1986), ch. 8. https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil375/Churchland.pdf
"Just as the obstetrician does not become pregnant by knowing all about pregnancy, so Mary does not have the sensation of redness by knowing all about the neurophysiology of perceiving and experiencing red."
"How can I assess what Mary will know and understand if she knows everything there is to know about the brain? Everything is a lot"

Paul Churchland, "Knowing Qualia" (1989): SECONDARY only. "a clear non sequitur"; the "ectoplasmologist" parallel (via Feser's blog).

## Rebukes of zombies

Yudkowsky, "Zombies: The Movie" (2008). https://www.readthesequences.com/Zombies-The-Movie
"Scientist: ... We're dealing with an epiphenomenal virus.
General Fred: Are you sure?
Scientist: As sure as we can be in the total absence of evidence.
General Fred: All right. Compile a report on every epiphenomenon ever observed. What, where, and who. I want a list of everything that hasn't happened in the last fifty years.
Captain Mudd: If the virus is epiphenomenal, how do we know it exists?
Scientist: The same way we know we're conscious.
Captain Mudd: Oh, okay."
Ending: "Colonel Todd: I don't feel anything. / The screen goes black. / The sound goes silent. / The movie continues exactly as before."

Yudkowsky, "Zombies! Zombies?" https://www.readthesequences.com/Zombies-Zombies
"the Zombie Argument may be a candidate for the most deranged idea in all of philosophy."
"To postulate this stuff of consciousness, and then further postulate that it doesn't do anything—for the love of cute kittens, why?"

Dennett, "The Unimagined Preposterousness of Zombies", JCS 2(4), 1995. https://dl.tufts.edu/downloads/rf55zm151?filename=6m312182x.pdf
"Sometimes philosophers clutch an insupportable hypothesis to their bosoms and run headlong over the cliff edge. Then, like cartoon characters, they hang there in mid-air, until they notice what they have done and gravity takes over."
"It seems to me that postulating zombies is exactly as silly as postulating epiphenomenal gremlins, and so when a philosopher does it, I blush for the profession."

Dennett, "The Zombic Hunch" (2001). https://dl.tufts.edu/downloads/9p290n11k?filename=44558r69g.pdf
"Must we talk about zombies? Apparently we must."
"Kangaroos are wonderful, but not that wonderful."

Hofstadter, I Am a Strange Loop (2007), pp. 322-323.
"The irony, of course, is that Universe Z's Dave Chalmers is lying through its teeth, yet without having the foggiest idea it's lying."
"...the zombie twin gives word for word and chortle for chortle the very same lecture, believing every word of it but not feeling a thing."

Smullyan, "An Unfortunate Dualist" (1980). https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~drkelly/SmullyanUnfortunateDualist1980.pdf
"'Damn it, this stuff hasn't helped at all! I still obviously have a soul and am suffering as much as ever!' Doesn't all this suggest that perhaps there might be something just a little wrong with dualism?"

Sean Carroll, "Zombies Must Be Dualists" (Nautilus, 2016). https://nautil.us/zombies-must-be-dualists-235983
"You don't think you're a zombie, but that's just what a zombie would say."
Blog, 2021: "Zombies would cry of heartbreak, compose happy songs, giggle while rolling around on the ground with puppies, and write densely-argued books against the idea that consciousness could be entirely physical."

Patricia Churchland, "The Hornswoggle Problem" (JCS, 1996). https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~drkelly/ChurchlandTheHornswoggleProblem1996.pdf
"none of the tendered conclusions follow, not even a little bit. Surrounded with rhetorical flourish, much brow furrowing and hand-wringing, however, versions of this argument can hornswoggle the unwary."

Dennett, Consciousness Explained p. 406 (SECONDARY): "Are zombies possible? They're not just possible, they're actual. We're all zombies." Footnote: "It would be an act of desperate intellectual dishonesty to quote this assertion out of context!"
