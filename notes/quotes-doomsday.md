# Doomsday and simulation (internal notes for docs/doomsday.html)

Collected 2026-10-11 by a research agent. Every quotation on the page was found by text search in the
source fetched from the URL given (arXiv and simulation-argument.com PDFs converted with pdftotext;
web pages stripped of markup) and copied from that text. Wayback copies were used where the live
site is behind Cloudflare. Web search was unavailable (budget exhausted), so sources came from known
URLs, arXiv, the Wayback CDX index and Bostrom's sites.

One page, not two. The simulation argument and Doomsday share the self-sampling move, and Bostrom
himself distinguishes them (2003, sec. V). That distinction is the hinge of the page, so it reads
better on one page.

### Bostrom (2003), "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?"
URL: https://www.simulation-argument.com/simulation.pdf (page numbers are the PDF's, not PQ's)
- Abstract: "This paper argues that at least one of the following propositions is true: (1) the
  human species is very likely to go extinct before reaching a “posthuman” stage; (2) any posthuman
  civilization is extremely unlikely to run a significant number of simulations of their
  evolutionary history (or variations thereof); (3) we are almost certainly living in a computer
  simulation."
- Sec. V: "The Doomsday argument rests on a much stronger and more controversial premiss, namely that
  one should reason as if one were a random sample from the set of all people who will ever have
  lived (past, present, and future) even though we know that we are living in the early twenty‐first
  century rather than at some point in the distant past or the future. The bland indifference
  principle, by contrast, applies only to cases where we have no information about which group of
  people we belong to."
- Sec. VII: "In the dark forest of our current ignorance, it seems sensible to apportion one's
  credence roughly evenly between (1), (2), and (3)."
- Sec. IV formula: f_sim = fP fI NI / (fP fI NI + 1).

### Caves (2008), arXiv:0806.3538
URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/0806.3538
- Abstract: "Nothing licenses indiscriminate use of Gott's rule as a predictor of future duration. It

- Sec. 4 (Conclusion), p. 10-11: "To sweeten the pot, I offered Gott 2:1 odds in his favor. Gott
  refused the bets on the grounds that “I don't do bets.”19 If he had believed his own predictions,

- Sec. 4, p. 11: "It is obvious that in a large sample of dogs selected without regard to age,
  roughly half the dogs, within the inevitable statistical fluctuations, will be in the first half

  half of their lives does not imply that any particular dog in the sample has a probability of 1/2

### Mortality check for Rebuke 1 (illustrative model, not a life table)
SSA's life table (ssa.gov) blocked the fetch and the Wayback Machine was offline, so a
Gompertz-Makeham model was used: hazard 0.0005 + 0.00003 exp(0.095 x), life expectancy 77.2 years.
Output: 50% interval coverage by age 1/10/20/40/60/80/90 = 0.001/0.026/0.477/0.958/0.579/0.001/0.000;
95% = 0.033/1.000/1.000/0.998/0.984/0.872/0.670; stationary-population averages 0.4997 and 0.9494.

```python
import numpy as np
A,B,c=5e-4,3e-5,0.095
x=np.linspace(0,130,130001); dx=x[1]-x[0]
H=A*x+B/c*(np.exp(c*x)-1); S=np.exp(-H)
print("life expectancy at birth", S.sum()*dx)
def surv(a,t): return np.interp(a+t,x,S,right=0)/np.interp(a,x,S)
for lvl,k in [(0.5,3),(0.95,39)]:
    for a in [0.1,1,5,10,20,40,60,80,90]:
        print(lvl, a, surv(a,a/k)-surv(a,a*k))
    ages=x[1:]; w=S[1:]/S[1:].sum()
    covs=np.array([surv(a,a/k)-surv(a,a*k) for a in ages[::100]])
    print("population-average", (covs*w[::100]).sum()/w[::100].sum())
```
Worth re-running against a real period life table before relying on the individual-age figures.
