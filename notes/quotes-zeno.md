# Zeno's paradoxes of motion: quotations used on docs/zeno.html (internal notes)

Checked 2026-10-11 against sources fetched in the session. All wording below matches the fetched text.

## Aristotle, Physics (Hardie and Gaye, Oxford 1930)
Source: Internet Classics Archive. The live site returned a stub page, so the text came from the Wayback copies:
https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.6.vi.html and .../physics.mb.txt (full text).
- VI.9 (239b5): "Zeno's reasoning, however, is fallacious, when he says that if everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if that which is in locomotion is always occupying such a space at any moment, the flying arrow is therefore motionless."
- VI.9 (239b8–9): "time is not composed of indivisible moments any more than any other magnitude is composed of indivisibles."
- VI.9 (239b9–13): "Zeno's arguments about motion, which cause so much disquietude to those who try to solve the problems that they present, are four in number. The first asserts the non-existence of motion on the ground that that which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal."
- VI.9 (239b14): "The second is the so-called 'Achilles', and it amounts to this, that in a race the quickest runner can never overtake the slowest, since the pursuer must first reach the point whence the pursued started, so that the slower must always hold a lead."
- VI.9 (Stadium): "the conclusion that half a given time is equal to double that time"; "a body occupies an equal time in passing with equal velocity a body that is in motion and a body of equal size that is at rest; which is false."
- VIII.8 (263a15): "But, although this solution is adequate as a reply to the questioner (the question asked being whether it is possible in a finite time to traverse or reckon an infinite number of units), nevertheless as an account of the fact and explanation of its true nature it is inadequate."
- Bekker numbers are standard locations (SEP gives 239b11 Dichotomy, 239b30 Arrow, 239b33 Stadium, 263a15). The fetched translation has no Bekker numbers; line-level numbers are approximate.

## Feynman Lectures, Vol. I §8-2
https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_08.html (curl and WebFetch got Cloudflare 403; read in the browser via Claude in Chrome, page text extracted)
- "The Greeks were somewhat confused by such problems, being helped, of course, by some very confusing Greeks."
- "What is wrong with that? It is that a finite amount of time can be divided into an infinite number of pieces, just as a length of line can be divided into an infinite number of pieces by dividing repeatedly by two. And so, although there are an infinite number of steps (in the argument) to the point at which Achilles reaches the tortoise, it doesn't mean that there is an infinite amount of time."
- Numbers used on the page (10x speed, 100 m head start) are Feynman's. The limit definition of velocity is later in §8-2.

## Russell, The Principles of Mathematics (1903, Cambridge UP)
https://archive.org/download/cu31924001610421/cu31924001610421_djvu.txt (OCR), cross-checked with principlesofmath005807mbp OCR.
- §327, pp. 347–348: "One of the most notable victims of posterity's lack of judgment is the Eleatic Zeno. Having invented four arguments, all immeasurably subtle and profound, the grossness of subsequent philosophers pronounced him to be a mere ingenious juggler, and his arguments to be one and all sophisms. After two thousand years of continual refutation, these sophisms were reinstated, and made the foundation of a mathematical renaissance, by a German professor, who probably never dreamed of any connection between himself and Zeno."
- §327: "has at last shown that we live in an unchanging world, and that the arrow, at every moment of its flight, is truly at rest." "(if he did infer)".
- Footnote to the end of §327, printed p. 348: "Not being a Greek scholar, I pretend to no first-hand authority as to what Zeno really did say or mean." Also "merely a text for discussion".
- §332, p. 350: "has usually been thought so monstrous a paradox as scarcely to deserve serious discussion"; "a very plain statement of a very elementary fact" (first OCR garbled; second OCR reads cleanly).
- §447, p. 473: "Motion consists merely in the occupation of different places at different times, subject to continuity as explained in Part V. There is no transition from place to place, no consecutive moment or consecutive position, no such thing as velocity except in the sense of a real number which is the limit of a certain set of quotients." OCR has "Set"; normalised to "set".
