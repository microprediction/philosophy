## Chalmers, The Conscious Mind (OUP 1996)
Source: archive.org scan, https://archive.org/details/chalmers-the-conscious-mind
(file Chalmers_The_Conscious_Mind.pdf, text layer extracted with pdftotext). OCR; punctuation checked by eye.

- p. 94: "So let us consider my zombie twin. This creature is molecule for molecule identical to me, and
  identical in all the low-level properties postulated by a completed physics, but he lacks conscious
  experience entirely. (Some might prefer to call a zombie "it," but I use the personal pronoun; I have
  grown quite fond of my zombie twin.)"
- p. 96: "Arguing for a logical possibility is not entirely straightforward. How, for example, would one
  argue that a mile-high unicycle is logically possible? It just seems obvious."
- p. 96: "I confess that the logical possibility of zombies seems equally obvious to me. A zombie is just
  something physically identical to me, but which has no conscious experience—all is dark inside. While
  this is probably empirically impossible, it certainly seems that a coherent situation is described; I
  can discern no contradiction in the description. In some ways an assertion of this logical possibility
  comes down to a brute intuition, but no more so than with the unicycle."
- p. 97 (paraphrased on page, not quoted): on the China-brain / silicon isomorph, "I suspect that in fact
  it would" be conscious.
- p. 177: "More strongly, it seems that consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and
  judgments about consciousness. This result I call the paradox of phenomenal judgment."
- p. 180: "To see the problem in a particularly vivid way, think of my zombie twin in the universe next
  door. He talks about conscious experience all the time—in fact, he seems obsessed by it. He spends
  ridiculous amounts of time hunched over a computer, writing chapter after chapter on the mysteries of
  consciousness."
- p. 180: "To strengthen the sense of paradox, note that my zombie twin is himself engaging in reasoning
  just like this."
- p. 181: "This paradoxical situation is at once delightful and disturbing. It is not obviously fatal to
  the nonreductive position, but it is at least something that we need to come to grips with. It is
  certainly the greatest tension that a nonreductive theory is faced with"
- p. 178 (context, paraphrased): the physical explanation of his claims holds "if the physical domain is
  causally closed".
- p. 190: "What might be going on when someone claims that introspection reveals only judgments? Perhaps
  Dennett is a zombie." Note 4 to ch. 5 (notes section, p. 379/380): "Of course, this would not really help."
  Also p. 190: Dennett's thesis "simply consists in a denial of the data that a theory of consciousness
  must explain."
- pp. 198–199: "Finally, there is a persistent refrain that comes up in these situations: "But your zombie
  twin would say the same thing!" ... To this, the answer is again, "So what?"" and "Despite the fact that
  he says the same things I do, I know that I am not him (though you might not be sure) because of my
  direct first-person acquaintance with my experiences."

## Chalmers, "Consciousness and its Place in Nature" (2003)
https://consc.net/papers/nature.html (also nature.pdf). Blackwell Guide to the Philosophy of Mind (2003);
reprinted in Chalmers (ed.), Philosophy of Mind (OUP 2002), pp. 247–72. Section 3 for the argument.
- The three-step argument, quoted verbatim: "(1) It is conceivable that there be zombies / (2) If it is
  conceivable that there be zombies, it is metaphysically possible that there be zombies. / (3) If it is
  metaphysically possible that there be zombies, then consciousness is nonphysical. / (4) Consciousness is
  nonphysical."
- "There is little reason to believe that zombies exist in the actual world. But many hold that they are
  at least conceivable: we can coherently imagine zombies, and there is no contradiction in the idea that
  reveals itself even on reflection."
- Section 7 (type-C), structure and dynamics: "First: Physical descriptions of the world characterize the
  world in terms of structure and dynamics. Second: From truths about structure and dynamics, one can
  deduce only further truths about structure and dynamics. And third: truths about consciousness are not
  truths about structure and dynamics."
- Section 6: type-B treats P⊃Q as "an a posteriori necessity, akin to such a posteriori necessities as
  'water is H2O'" (paraphrased with that phrase).

## Chalmers, "The Two-Dimensional Argument Against Materialism"
https://consc.net/papers/2dargument.html (pre-publication version; in The Character of Consciousness, OUP 2010).
- Section 2: "(2) Where S is a highly complex but provable mathematical truth, ~S will be prima facie
  conceivable for most subjects, but it is not ideally conceivable"
- "Insofar as there is a gap between prima facie conceivability and ideal conceivability, it is ideal
  conceivability that is a better guide to possibility."
- Objection (5) "The judgments of zombies": "It is an admittedly strange feature of zombies that they
  appear to make claims about consciousness that are indistinguishable from the claims of conscious beings"
  and "while it is strange (many strange things happen in other possible worlds), it does nothing to
  undermine the coherence of zombies."
- Objection (2) zombie parity (Balog): "in a debate between zombie realists and zombie eliminativists, the
  zombie eliminativists (who say 'No-one is phenomenally conscious') are correct."
- Objection (15) conceivability of materialism (Marton, Sturgeon): "It may be prima facie negatively
  conceivable that materialism is true about consciousness, but it is not obviously conceivable in any
  stronger sense." and "I do not think this unimaginability is so obvious that it should be used as a
  premise in an argument against materialism, but likewise, the imaginability claim cannot be used as a
  premise either."
- Objection (4): interactionist dualism compatible with zombies, which would have "large causal gaps in
  their functioning" (paraphrased).

## Chalmers, "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness", JCS 25(9–10), 2018, pp. 6–61
https://consc.net/papers/metaproblem.pdf (pdftotext per page; pdf page 42 = journal p. 47).
- p. 47: "Still, there unquestionably remains some discomfort in the vicinity."
- p. 48: "in the case of consciousness one has a very strong sort of modal independence, in that there
  seems to be a near-complete structural explanation of the intuitions that could obtain without
  consciousness." (used in paraphrase/short quote)
- pp. 55–56: Chalmers quotes Yudkowsky (2008) at length, including "the Zombie Argument may be a
  candidate for the most deranged idea in all of philosophy".
- p. 56: "I think that, as things stand, neither illusionism nor realism has a truly satisfactory
  response to the charge of absurdity."
- p. 14 n. 8: PhilPapers Survey (Bourget and Chalmers 2014): "36% of the target
  group judge that zombies are conceivable but not metaphysically possible, 16% judge that they are
  inconceivable, and 23% judge that they are metaphysically impossible". CAUTION: "23% ... metaphysically impossible" is as printed; presumably
  means "metaphysically possible". Page uses only the 36% and 16% figures.

## Frankish, "The Anti-Zombie Argument", Philosophical Quarterly 57(229), 2007, 650–666
E-print from Open Research Online, fetched via web.archive.org:
http://oro.open.ac.uk/2191/1/Antizombie_eprint_rev.pdf (direct link blocked by Cloudflare; archived copy
fetched). Quotes from the e-print, which "may differ in minor ways from the print version".
- "I shall call an object x a bare physical duplicate of an object y if x is a physical duplicate of y and
  has no further properties of a non-physical kind."
- "My anti-zombie twin is just something which is physically identical with me, and has no non-physical
  properties, but which has conscious experience — the lights are on inside."
- "Chalmers himself proposes that neither the conceivability of physicalism nor its inconceivability
  should be used as a premise in argument, but I see no reason to accept this ban. If the claim that
  zombies are conceivable is admissible as a premise, then why should the parallel claim about
  anti-zombies not be admissible too?"
- "They cannot invoke the zombie argument itself, on pain of begging the question."
- "When zombies and anti-zombies meet, they annihilate each other, and in so doing reveal that
  considerations of conceivability have little role to play in debates about the nature of consciousness."
- Acknowledgement thanks Chalmers for comments on drafts.
- I did not find a reply by Chalmers addressed to Frankish by name in the texts fetched. The page says so
  and gives his reply to the parallel Marton/Sturgeon argument.

## Dennett, "The Unimagined Preposterousness of Zombies", JCS 2(4), 1995, 322–326
https://dl.tufts.edu/downloads/rf55zm151?filename=6m312182x.pdf (OCR scan)
- p. 322: "when philosophers claim that zombies are conceivable, they invariably underestimate the task of
  conception (or imagination), and end up imagining something that violates their own definition."
- p. 322: zimbo "by definition a zombie equipped for higher-order reflective informational states"
- p. 323: "they are just 'wrong' (according to this lamentable tradition), in ways that neither they nor
  we could ever discover!" (Preceding words use Dennett's subscripted "think_Z", garbled in OCR; not quoted.)
- p. 326: "postulating zombies is exactly as silly as postulating epiphenomenal gremlins, and so
  when a philosopher does it, I blush for the profession." (verified; page inferred from scan order, pdf page 6)
