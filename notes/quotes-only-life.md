# "Every X we know is Y, so X needs Y" (internal notes)

Collected 2026-10-11 by a research agent. Unless a block is marked NOT VERIFIED or SECONDARY, every
quotation below was found by text search in the source fetched from the URL given (PDFs converted
with pdftotext; web pages stripped of markup) and copied from that text. Typos and spellings are
the authors'. Page numbers are printed page numbers unless marked "pdf p.".

The argument on this page: every known case of X (consciousness, intelligence, flight, organic
chemistry, tool-making) is Y (alive, embodied, biological, human), so X (probably) requires Y.
The sample is drawn from one lineage on one planet. The centrepiece is Seth's consciousness/life
version. Other instances, AI and historical, follow.

  "if only for the simple reason"). He never says the induction proves BN. Quote the hedge.
- In his BBS Response, Seth rejects Michel's induction for functionalism as having an
  "unsupported" inductive step (below). That is the same move he makes for biology. Useful, but

- Block is on both sides. In 2002 he says a different physical realization is "not a ground of
  rational belief" that a system lacks consciousness. In 2025-26 he argues that biology
  ("meat") may be necessary. Both are quoted. Neither is a straight version of the induction.
  In his 2026 review of Pollan, Block uses the "only entities we are sure are conscious" premise
  against *plant* consciousness, not AI.
- Godfrey-Smith states the premise ("all the systems we encounter that are clear and uncontested
  cases of systems with minds are also living systems") as one of two "obvious facts", then
  says their status is uncertain. The wording comes from the 2014 NYU talk version on his
  website. The published J. Phil. 2016 version may be worded differently. Check before quoting
  the J. Phil. page.
- Searle 1980 is the ancestor, but he explicitly leaves Martians open as "an empirical

- Wöhler (1828) did not kill vitalism by himself. Remsen (1875, below) is a good period source
  for how slowly the "organic only from organisms" dogma fell back: first to "cyanate came from
  animals", then to "complicated compounds need the vital force". That slow retreat is the
  parallel worth drawing.
- Newcomb's essay was written after Langley's failed trial (Oct 1903) and before the Wright
  flight (17 Dec 1903). The text below is from the 1906 book reprint. The book says the essay is
  "mainly from The New York Independent, but in part from McClure's Magazine". The Independent

## Defendants

### Anil Seth (2026), "Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 49, e315

https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032 (target article accepted 21 March 2025; manuscript online
21 April 2025; treatment with commentaries and Response, 2026). Text: `seth_raw.txt`.

Target article, section 7.0 Conclusions, p. 15 (line 1668):
"There are good reasons to think that consciousness may not be possible at all in conventional AI systems, and there are at least some good (non-vitalistic) reasons to suppose that consciousness may be a property of, and perhaps only of, living systems. Simply noting that all known examples of conscious systems are biological and alive suggests that biological naturalism is no more speculative than computational functionalism."

Author's Response, R7 Summary, p. 106 (line 11858). This is the main defence, and it repeats and strengthens the inference:
"First, as things stand today, we should take BN rather than CF to be the default position. This is because (i) there are good reasons, grounded in our own psychology, why we are likely to overattribute consciousness to some forms of AI (CAIBN 2.0, R6); (ii) there are good reasons to challenge the assumption that substrate-independent computation is sufficient for consciousness (CAIBN 3.0, R2, R3); (iii) there are increasingly many non-vitalistic, non-chauvinistic reasons why biological properties might be necessary for brain function in general and for consciousness in particular (CAIBN 4.0, R4, R5); and (iv) the only systems we are presently sure are conscious are living systems – setting an empirical prior."
[pdftotext breaks "substrateindependent" and "nonchauvinistic" across lines; hyphens restored.]

Response, R1.1 Preliminaries, p. 93 (line 10470), the "asymmetry" he leans on:
"Second, the bets placed by CF and BN are asymmetric. CF is a claim about sufficiency across substrates. This imposes a high evidential burden which I believe remains unmet; CF mostly remains assumed rather than explicitly argued for (R2, R3). BN is the logically lighter claim that some biological substrate properties are necessary for consciousness"
[p. 93 by footer count.]

Response to Michel, R2.1 Defending CF, p. 94 (line 10551). Seth rejects an induction for functionalism. Compare it with (iv) above:
"Fourth, and related: Michel's argument is a variety of induction, but the key inductive step is unsupported. While psychological capacities like memory and language can, in some form, be uncontroversially characterised in terms of (potentially computational) functions, the same cannot be said for consciousness. Assuming that computationalist explanations will work as well for the latter as for the former requires assuming that consciousness is relevantly similar – i.e., that computations are sufficient – which is precisely the claim at issue."

Response to Richards & Agüera y Arcas on flight, R3.3, p. 96 (line 10780):
"While being substrate independent increases the plausibility that a function is computational, not all substrate independent/flexible functions are computational (flight, again, is a good example). Moreover, while flight has a consensus functional description – broadly, anything that moves through the air in a controlled and sustained way – consciousness does not."

## Other instances: historical, later refuted

### Vitalism: organic compounds only from living things. Ira Remsen, "The Artificial Preparation of Organic Bodies", Popular Science Monthly 7, October 1875

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_7/October_1875/The_Artificial_Preparation_of_Organic_Bodies
(Remsen, then professor of chemistry at Williams College, later co-discoverer of saccharin and
president of Johns Hopkins.) A period chemist describes the induction and its retreat.

The induction:
"Up to within a few years chemists were, to a certain extent, justified in drawing a line of division between two classes of bodies, both occurring in Nature: 1. Those which can be prepared in the laboratory; 2. Those which cannot be prepared in the laboratory. The second class included the so-called organic bodies. These were known to occur only in the organs of plants or animals. The two facts, taken together, were significant, and but little surprise can be expressed that a connection was traced between them. The simplest conclusion that could be drawn from the premises was drawn, and the scientific world, buoyed up by certain preconceived notions in regard to life, tacitly accepted it. Chemical substances which are produced under the mysterious influence of life, in the dark, unfathomed cavities of living organisms, cannot be produced by the hand of the mortal chemist. This was the conclusion which grew to be a dogma. and was used as a kind of ex post facto argument in favor of certain views in regard to the so-called "vital force.""

Wöhler, 1828:
"Urea was well known, but had, up to that time, only been found among the products formed in animal bodies. Its existence was, in accordance with the then prevalent views, supposed to be due to the inexplicable action of the vital force. A careful examination failed to disclose any points of difference between the two bodies, and Wöhler was forced to the conclusion that at least one organic substance could be prepared outside of the organism."

First retreat:
"But this by no means brought about a change of views. The upholders of the old dogma immediately found relief which was apparently satisfactory. Cyanogen compounds, of course including cyanic acid, had only been prepared from substances which had had their origin in the organs of animals, and, although these original substances had been subjected to purely chemical influences, and thus another animal substance produced, the vital force had nevertheless played its part as an essential agent in the formative process."

Second retreat:
"It was now stated that, although it might be possible to prepare artificially some of the simpler organic bodies, the vital force was necessary to bring about the complicated form of union found in the greater number of the products of the life-process. This statement held its own for a number of years."
And: "Surely the vital force alone could build up more complicated bodies. Not so."

End:
"The old dogma no longer exists."

Wöhler's letter to Berzelius, 22 February 1828: "I must tell you that I can make urea without
the use of kidneys, either man or dog" (common English rendering). NOT VERIFIED. The 1901
Briefwechsel zwischen J. Berzelius und F. Wöhler, ed. Wallach, vol. 1 has the letter. Only vol. 2
was found on archive.org (briefwechselzwis02berzuoft), so the German wording was not checked.

## Rebuttals

### In the BBS treatment itself

Dung, "Why I am not a biological naturalist", e331, p. 43-44 (lines 4970, 5040):
"However, it is also important to note that biological naturalism is a stronger claim than that AI consciousness is impossible: It also rules out consciousness in creatures, for example, hypothetical extraterrestrials, which are not made from silicon but which also do not qualify as living creatures."
p. 44: "Finally, biological naturalism specifically is subject to the charge that restricting consciousness to biological systems requires positing that it is an implausible coincidence that humans are conscious (see Schwitzgebel & Pober, 2024)."

Hohwy, "Self-evidencing and consciousness-specific mortal computation", e339, p. 55 (line 6261):
"The argument is powerful but notice that it rests on the assumption that the difference between mortal and immortal computation is relevant to consciousness. This assumption should be substantiated, because it might just be that consciousness depends on something else than computation, whether mortal or immortal – the fact that we are conscious and that our brains are mortal computers could be a coincidence, after all."

Bayne, "Biases and the question of artificial consciousness", e319, p. 27 (line 3125), on Bisson's "They're made out of meat":
"Are Bisson's imagined visitors guilty of the bias of chauvinism (anthropomorphism's twin) in doubting the possibility of human consciousness because we're made of meat and not silicon? If so, are we guilty of chauvinism in wondering whether silicon-based systems could be conscious? Perhaps. Equally, one could argue that both inferences are reasonable (albeit potentially flawed) implications of the fact that when faced with alien systems, one has no alternative but to extrapolate from oneself."

Chrisley, "Conscious AI does not require computational functionalism, and consciousness may be non-biological, like flight", e327, p. 39 (line 4397). He re-runs Seth's own sentences with "flight" in place of "consciousness":
"Despite detailing the intimate way in which human consciousness is interleaved with, and constrained by, our biology, Seth fails to show that this is necessarily essential to all consciousness. Consider flight: For living fliers (e.g., birds), "the connections between properties of the biological substrate and [particular instances of flying] have rich potential explanatory value. ..." And: "Flight in biological systems is a dynamical and (plausibly) substrate-dependent process." Do these facts imply that flight in general is best thought of as a biological phenomenon? Do they imply that artificial flight is impossible? Do they imply that artificial flight can only be achieved by building systems that closely emulate this biological complexity? Obviously not."

Shevlin, "Aeroplanes also fly: Analytic functionalism and the possibility of machine consciousness", e362, p. 87 (line 9822):
"As an initial analogy, consider the phenomenon of flight. Over the course of evolution, flight has emerged independently at least four times, in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats. A proper understanding of flight in each case will make essential reference to fine-grained biological characteristics, from the demands it makes on metabolism to its associated and varied changes in muscles, tendons, and skeletal structure. However, we also know that flight is not restricted to biological organisms: human artefacts such as planes, helicopters, and rockets demonstrate that flight can be achieved in quite different forms."

Bowes, "Birds do it, we do it, even embodied machines could do it. Why consciousness is not intrinsically biological.", e324, p. 35 (lines 4002, 4026):
"The claim that consciousness came about through natural processes, and that carbon-based life on Earth is one way this could happen, is just naturalism."
"The additional claim of necessity depends on an assumption that only biological entities can have the suitable properties, which has not been established."

Michel, "Bet on functionalism", e348, p. 67 (line 7550). The counter-induction, which Seth answers above:
"First, functionalism wins on induction. When we explain other psychological capacities – mental arithmetic, language production, causal inference, memory retrieval, etc. – we do it functionally. ... By induction, we should expect that the difference between conscious and unconscious mental states is also a matter of functional role."

Allen, "Too soon to say what consciousness is", e316, abstract, p. 22 (line 2500):
"No one is in a position to say what really matters for consciousness, or whether only living systems can instantiate the complexity needed for conscious feelings."

### Jeremy Pober and Eric Schwitzgebel, "Substrate Flexibility and the Copernican Principle of Consciousness" (draft, 28 May 2026)

http://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/SubstrateFlexibility-260528.pdf
(abstract page http://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzAbs/SubstrateFlexibility.htm). It names
Seth (forthcoming), Block 2025 and Cao 2022 as targets. This is the cleanest "one planet" rebuttal.

Abstract, p. 1:
"Some recent critiques of standard arguments for the substrate flexibility of consciousness (e.g., Cao 2022; Block 2025; Seth forthcoming) have emphasized that humanlike consciousness might require our specific biological substrate. However, such critiques are too narrowly focused to address the issue of consciousness in entities whose experience may be very different from ours, for example alien life forms or future AI systems designed along unfamiliar lines. Given that it's likely that functionally complex, behaviorally sophisticated entities have arisen or will arise many times in the observable universe, in diverse substrates, we argue that it would be a violation of a principle of Copernican mediocrity to hold that among these diverse entities, only we, or only we and a small proportion of others who share our substrate, are conscious."

p. 15:
"The Copernican Principle of Consciousness: Among behaviorally sophisticated entities, we are not specially privileged with respect to consciousness (Schwitzgebel and Pober 2026)."
"If the Copernican Principle of Consciousness is correct, we are not specially lucky to possess consciousness-instilling Earthiform biology while other behaviorally sophisticated entities' architectures leave them entirely nonconscious. ... Absent some reason to think we are special, we Earthlings would then be suspiciously, inexplicably lucky."

p. 16:
"Our substrate is, of course, somewhat special: here on Earth, it alone supports behaviorally sophisticated, conscious life. But among other substrates in the universe that can support behaviorally sophisticated life and may be favored in environments unlike ours with different resources and constraints, we have no reason to believe that our substrate is uniquely capable of supporting consciousness. Without such a positive reason, believing our substrate special in this way would again violate a plausible principle of mediocrity."

[In their favour of the defendants: Pober and Schwitzgebel's earlier paper, "The Copernican
Argument for Alien Consciousness; The Mimicry Argument Against Robot Consciousness" (arXiv
2412.00008), argues that the Copernican argument does NOT carry over to AI built to mimic us.
This one argues for substrate flexibility among evolved aliens, not for LLM consciousness.]

### David Chalmers (2023), "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?", Boston Review, 9 August 2023

arXiv version: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103 (NeurIPS talk of 28 Nov 2022, edited).
pdf p. 7:

In earlier work, I've argued that these views involve a sort of biological chauvinism and should be rejected. In my view, silicon is just as apt as carbon as a substrate for consciousness. What matters is how neurons or silicon chips are hooked up to each other, not what they are made of."
Footnote, pdf p. 15 (fairness: he gives the biology view real weight):
"The future-AI figures might tend to suggest a collective credence of at least one in three that biology is required for consciousness (albeit among philosophers rather than consciousness researchers)."

### David Chalmers (1995), "Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia", in T. Metzinger (ed.), Conscious Experience, Imprint Academic

https://consc.net/papers/qualia.html (the "earlier work" he cites in 2023: the gradual silicon
replacement argument).
Section 2:
"The principle of organizational invariance is far from universally accepted. Some have thought it likely that for a system to be conscious it must have the right sort of biochemical makeup; if so, a metallic robot or a silicon-based computer could never have experiences, no matter what its causal organization."
Fading qualia conclusion:
"A much more reasonable hypothesis is therefore that when neurons are replaced, qualia do not fade at all. A system like Joe, in practice, will have conscious experiences just as rich as mine. If so, then our original assumption was wrong, and the original isomorph, Robot, has conscious experiences."
His own limit on the argument, section 1:
"These arguments do not constitute conclusive proof of the principle of organizational invariance. Such proof is generally not available in the domain of conscious experience, where for familiar reasons one cannot even disprove the hypothesis that there is only one conscious being."

### Ned Block (2002), "The Harder Problem of Consciousness", Journal of Philosophy 99(8), 391-425

Author copy linked from https://www.nedblock.us/publications (Google Drive id 1Lfe6yq9LgXZ4KSdpQu6QuBXpaTCbuhYs; JSTOR scan).
p. 405:
"(C3): Fundamentally different physical realization from us per se is not a ground of rational belief in lack of consciousness. So the fact that Commander Data's control mechanisms are fundamentally different is not a ground of rational belief that he has no phenomenal states. Note that I do not say that finding out that Commander Data has a silicon-based brain is not a reason for regarding him as lacking consciousness. Rather, I say that the reason falls below the epistemic level of a ground for rational belief."
"(C4): We have no conception of a ground of rational belief to the effect that a realization of our superficial functional organization that is physically fundamentally different along the lines I have specified for Commander Data is or is not conscious."
[Block's point cuts both ways: the sample of known conscious things cannot settle the question
either way. That is the case against "simply noting".]

### Butlin, Long, et al. (2023), "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness", arXiv:2308.08708

https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708. Neutral. Use it to show the biology view is taken seriously, and why.
p. 14:
"We adopt computational functionalism as a working hypothesis primarily for pragmatic reasons. ... On the other hand, if computational functionalism is false, there is no guarantee that computational features which are correlated with consciousness in humans will be good indicators of consciousness in AI. It could be, for instance, that some non-computational feature of living organisms is necessary for consciousness (Searle 1980, Seth 2021), in which case consciousness would be impossible in non-organic artificial systems."
p. 42 (summarising Godfrey-Smith and Man & Damasio):
"On this basis, Godfrey-Smith suggests that artificial systems can only bear coarse-grained functional similarities to living organisms and that these will not be sufficient for consciousness. So another proposed necessary condition is that a conscious system must undergo metabolic processes realised at the nanoscale. Man and Damasio (2019) also suggest that self-maintenance, and thus consciousness, may depend on systems' specific material composition."
