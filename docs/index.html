Philosophy is Mostly F#@king Dumb

Rebukes to classic arguments that have been getting away with it for far too long.

Some philosophical arguments are famous because they are good. Others are famous because they are memorable — a man in a room, a colour-blind scientist, a very large bet on God — and the vividness of the story has been doing the work the premises were supposed to do. Those arguments get anthologised, assigned to undergraduates, and cited for decades as though they had settled something.

This site is where I get them off my chest. Each entry takes one celebrated argument, states it as fairly as I can manage, and then explains why it doesn't work. The tone is not reverent. The arguments are meant to be.

Rebukes

  • The Chinese Room
    John Searle, 1980 · philosophy of mind

    A man with a rulebook shuffles Chinese symbols and understands nothing, so no program ever could. It's a trick: the argument trades on our intuitions about books, and any book that could actually do the job would be bigger than a great many universes. We have no intuitions about those.

Awaiting trial, in no particular order. Appearing here is an accusation, not a conviction — a few of these may yet be acquitted.

  • Mary's Room — Jackson, 1982
  • Philosophical zombies — Chalmers, 1996
  • The Gödel argument against mechanism — Lucas, 1961; Penrose, 1989
  • Pascal's Wager — Pascal, 1670
  • The ontological argument — Anselm, 1078, and a long line of repeat offenders
  • The Doomsday argument — Carter; Leslie; Gott
  • The simulation argument — Bostrom, 2003
