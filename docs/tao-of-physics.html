The Tao of Physics

He hitched a religion to a theory of physics. The theory lost the year before he published.

Defendant
Fritjof Capra, The Tao of Physics (1975), and the afterwords of 1983 and 1991
Claim
Modern physics and Eastern mysticism have arrived at the same view of the world, and the bootstrap theory of particles is where the two meet.
Charge
Treating similarities of English translation as evidence of a shared discovery, and then declining to notice when the physics changed.
Verdict
Guilty. Not for the bet he placed in 1974, which was defensible, but for the two afterwords insisting he had not lost it.

The argument, in his words

It opens on a beach:

I ‘saw’ cascades of energy coming down from outer space, in which particles were created and destroyed in rhythmic pulses; I ‘saw’ the atoms of the elements and those of my body participating in this cosmic dance of energy; I felt its rhythm and I ‘heard’ its sound, and at that moment I knew that this was the Dance of Shiva, the Lord of Dancers worshipped by the Hindus. — Capra, preface (1975)

He is candid about the circumstances, which is to his credit: “In the beginning, I was helped on my way by ‘power plants’ which showed me how the mind can flow freely.” The thesis arrives in chapter 1:

We shall see how the two foundations of twentieth-century physics—quantum theory and relativity theory—both force us to see the world very much in the way a Hindu, Buddhist or Taoist sees it … we shall often encounter statements where it is almost impossible to say whether they have been made by physicists or by Eastern mystics. — Capra, ch. 1

The parallels turn up, he says, in the Vedas, the I Ching, the Buddhist sutras, Heraclitus, Sufism, and “the teachings of the Yaqui sorcerer Don Juan”. The book builds to Geoffrey Chew’s bootstrap theory, in which no particle is elementary and each is held together by the others:

The bootstrap philosophy constitutes the final rejection of the mechanistic world view in modern physics. … this view of nature came ever closer to the Eastern world view and is now in harmony with Eastern thought, both in its general philosophy and in its specific picture of matter. — Capra, ch. 18

The Buddhist image of Indra’s net, in which every jewel reflects every other, “may justly be called the first bootstrap model, created by the Eastern sages some 2,500 years before the beginning of particle physics.”

Rebuke 1: the method finds parallels wherever it looks

The book’s procedure is to set a sentence by a physicist beside a sentence from a sutra and invite you to notice how alike they sound. Jeremy Bernstein, a physicist writing in the American Scholar, put his finger on it:

At the heart of the matter is Mr. Capra’s methodology—his use of what seem to me to be accidental similarities of language as if these were somehow evidence of deeply rooted connections. — Bernstein (1978)

He offered a riddle by way of demonstration:

What did Abraham Lincoln and Albert Einstein have in common? Answer: They both had beards except for Einstein. — Bernstein (1978)

Isaac Asimov put the objection in terms of supply. The corpus of sage-sayings is enormous, the sayings are short, and the translations are loose, so a reader who already knows the answer can always find something that fits it:

Anyone as imaginative and dedicated as Fritjof Capra (or even someone more limited, such as myself) can go through the vast volume of Eastern sage-sayings and come up with remarks that can be interpreted to seem to match any scientific conclusion. — Asimov (1980)

And he asked the question the whole genre never answers. If the texts contain the physics, the physics should have been readable off them in advance.

It would be much simpler to study Taoist texts for the answers. But if Taoist texts can only be properly understood after physicists reach the answers, then of what scientific use are the Taoist texts? — Asimov (1980)

That is the test, and the book fails it. Not one of the parallels was drawn before the physics was known. Every one was found afterwards, by someone holding the answer.

A demonstration

Asserting that is cheap. Here is the method run against its author, which is not. Capra’s central parallel, the one the book builds to, is that Eastern thought denies fundamental constituents:

The world view of the Eastern mystics shares with the bootstrap philosophy of modern physics not only an emphasis on the mutual interrelation and self-consistency of all phenomena, but also the denial of fundamental constituents of matter. — Capra, ch. 18

Now the same procedure, pointed the other way. From the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on classical Indian atomism:

Two distinct systems that regard Vedic texts as authoritative, Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika, favoured atomist accounts of the material world. … The Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika theory posited different kinds of atoms for each of the elements earth, water, air and fire … Atoms combine into dyads and triads before they aggregate into bodies of a kind that can be perceived. — SEP, “Ancient Atomism”

Indivisible particles. Of distinct kinds. Combining in twos and in threes, before anything observable appears. A meson is two quarks and a baryon is three, and the encyclopedia dates the doctrine several centuries before the common era.

So the sages anticipated the quark model: the theory Capra says cannot be right, in a tradition he says denies fundamental constituents, matched on the one structural detail that distinguishes it. His own thesis, run backwards, using his own method.

I do not believe a word of it, and nor should anyone. It took one encyclopedia entry, and the fit is closer than anything in the book, which offers Oppenheimer on the electron beside an Upanishad on a thing that “moves” and “moves not”. A method that yields the answer you brought with it is not evidence. That is the whole objection, and it is why the parallels never arrive before the physics.

Rebuke 2: the theory he chose had already lost

The bootstrap was a real research programme, and in the 1960s a respectable one. It held that there are no elementary constituents at all. Its rival held that hadrons are made of quarks.

Capra picked a side, in print:

At present, in the summer of 1974, the paradoxes surrounding the quark model are becoming increasingly sharp. … No one has ever seen a quark, and according to our basic ideas about particle interactions quarks cannot exist. — Capra, ch. 16 (1st edition)

Here is the timetable. Asymptotic freedom, the result that made quantum chromodynamics work, was proved in 1973. In November 1974 two groups found the J/ψ particle, which was promptly understood as a quark bound to its antiquark. Capra finished the book in London in December 1974. It was published in 1975.

The discovery did reach him, as a footnote on the same page: “The recently discovered ‘psi particles’ may be of that kind.” A sentence declaring that quarks cannot exist, and a footnote reporting the experiment that showed they do, sit on one page of the first edition.

That much is bad luck rather than bad faith. Quark scepticism was a respectable position in the summer of 1974, and a book finished in December cannot absorb an experiment from November. The trouble starts afterwards.

Rebuke 3: two afterwords, sixteen years, no correction

By 1983 the standard model had won. The second edition’s preface reports otherwise: “It has been very gratifying for me that none of these recent developments has invalidated anything I wrote seven years ago.” Its afterword blames the profession:

Yet, in spite of all these difficulties, most physicists still hang on to the idea of basic building blocks of matter which is so deeply ingrained in our Western scientific tradition. — Capra, afterword to the second edition (1983)

He also notes that “not a single Nobel prize has so far been awarded to any of the outstanding physicists who contributed to the development of S-matrix theory”, and maintains that “our present understanding of the subatomic world precludes the existence of quarks as physical particles”. Peter Woit, a mathematical physicist not noted for gentleness toward the mainstream either:

What is harder to understand is that the book has now gone through several editions, and in each of them Capra has left intact the now out-of-date physics, including new forewords and afterwords that with a straight face deny what has happened. — Woit, Not Even Wrong (2006)

Victor Stenger’s verdict on the theory itself is four words long: “bootstrap theory simply did not work.” Capra’s 1991 afterword explains the critics instead: they “would react with anger… which reflected their own insecurity”. And then the boast:

I am very pleased that in all the criticism I have had from fellow physicists, not one of them has found any fault in my presentation of the concepts of modern physics. … to the best of my knowledge nobody has found any factual errors in The Tao of Physics. — Capra, afterword to the third edition (1991)

Rebuke 4: the sentence Bohr wrote next

Since he asks. The book leans on the founders of quantum mechanics, who did remark on resemblances to Eastern thought, and it quotes them accurately as far as it goes. The trouble is where it stops.

Here is the Bohr passage Capra uses:

For a parallel to the lesson of atomic theory regarding the limited applicability of such customary idealisations, we must in fact turn to quite other branches of science, such as psychology, or even to that kind of epistemological problems with which already thinkers like Buddha and Lao Tse have been confronted … — Bohr, “Biology and Atomic Physics” (1937)

And here is the sentence that follows it in Bohr:

Still, the recognition of an analogy in the purely logical character of the problems which present themselves in so widely separated fields of human interest does in no way imply acceptance in atomic physics of any mysticism foreign to the true spirit of science … — Bohr, the next sentence

Bohr anticipated the use his remark would be put to, and wrote the disclaimer immediately underneath it. The quotation stops one sentence early. That is a factual error about the concepts of modern physics, in the exact sense the author invited, and it has been sitting in every edition since 1975.

Rebuke 5: the hostage he gave

The deepest objection came from the mystical side of the aisle. Ken Wilber, who is not a materialist and not unsympathetic to Capra, pointed out what tying a religion to a live theory costs:

If today’s physics supports mysticism, what happens when tomorrow’s physics replaces it? Does mysticism then fall also? We cannot have it both ways. — Wilber, Quantum Questions (1984)

And, on the bootstrap specifically: “But nowadays virtually all physicists believe there are irreducible things (quarks, leptons, gluons) that arise out of broken symmetries. Does Buddha therefore lose his enlightenment?”

Wilber adds the reductio. If physics and mysticism are two routes to one destination, “why bother with years of arduous meditation? Just read The Tao of Physics.” Bernstein reached the same place from the other direction: “To hitch a religious philosophy to a contemporary science is a sure route to its obsolescence.”

Twenty-eight years on, Capra conceded the physics. His memorial notice for Chew, in 2019, says it plainly: “Today, bootstrap physics has virtually disappeared from the scene.” The afterwords have never been revised.

The descendants

The book sold in the millions across forty-three editions and twenty-three languages, and one reviewer credits it with starting a genre. Leon Lederman, a Nobel laureate, filed the genre under a chapter heading, “The Dancing Moo-Shu Masters”, and was careful to separate Capra from what followed:

And there’s certainly nothing wrong with finding parallels between the new quantum physics and Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism, Zen, or Hunan cuisine, for that matter. … In fact, Tao and Wu Li constitute a relatively respectable middle ground between good science books and a lunatic fringe of fakes, charlatans, and crazies. These folks guarantee eternal life if you restrict your diet to sumac roots. — Lederman, The God Particle (1993)

The lunatic fringe arrived on schedule. In 1998 Deepak Chopra took the Ig Nobel Prize in physics “for his unique interpretation of quantum physics as it applies to life, liberty, and the pursuit of economic happiness”. In 2004 the film What the Bleep Do We Know!? put the philosopher of physics David Albert on screen as a supporter. Albert:

I am, indeed, profoundly unsympathetic to attempts at linking quantum mechanics with consciousness. Moreover, I explained all that, at great length, on camera, to the producers of the film … Had I known that I would have been so radically misrepresented in the movie, I would certainly not have agreed to be filmed. — David Albert (2004)

The genre’s final honour came from Alan Sokal, whose 1996 hoax paper recommends “the book by Capra (1975)… for its scientific accuracy and its accessibility to non-specialists”. The editors did not notice the joke.

Scope

None of this shows that the physics in the book is wrong where it is doing physics. The critics who read it closely say the opposite, and so does this page.

Nor does it show that mysticism is false, or that the resemblances are imaginary. Sentences about the unity of things do occur in both literatures. The objection is to the inference from resemblance to shared discovery.

And Capra is not Chopra. He does not say that consciousness collapses wave functions or that quantum mechanics cures illness. His epilogue asks whether physicists should meditate and whether a synthesis is possible, and answers: “I think all these questions have to be answered in the negative.”

In his favour

The book is well written and its physics exposition is good, which is why so many physicists recommended it. Lederman grants that Capra “got a lot of things right” and that there is “some good physics writing” in it. Bernstein concedes that “a close reading of his book—especially the epilogue—indicates that he is aware of the limitations of the parallels he attempts to draw”. Wilber calls him “one of the most careful of the new-age writers”.

The founders really did make these comparisons themselves. Bohr put a yin-yang on his coat of arms. Schrödinger wrote about the Upanishads in What Is Life? Oppenheimer wrote that the notions illustrated by atomic physics have “in Buddhist and Hindu thought a more considerable and central place”, and—in a passage Capra quotes and then cuts short—compared the electron answers to the Buddha’s. That truncation went against Capra’s own interest, which suggests carelessness rather than dishonesty.

And the closing line of the book is the right one, whatever leads up to it: “Science does not need mysticism and mysticism does not need science; but man needs both.” The objection is not to wanting both. It is to claiming they had found the same thing.
