Every Known Example

Every conscious thing we know of is alive. Every one is also made of carbon, evolved on Earth and was picked out by its resemblance to us.

Defendants
Anil Seth (2026); Ned Block (2026); with Simon Newcomb (1903) and the vitalist chemists as precedent
Claim
“All known examples of conscious systems are biological and alive”, so consciousness probably requires life.
Charge
Sampling one lineage on one planet, choosing the sample by its likeness to us, and reading what it has in common as a requirement.
Verdict
Guilty of the inference. The conclusion may still be true. Life may matter for consciousness, but a list of the conscious things we already recognise cannot show it.

The argument, in their words

Anil Seth’s target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences argues that consciousness may depend on being alive. Its conclusion uses the argument this page is about:

Simply noting that all known examples of conscious systems are biological and alive suggests that biological naturalism is no more speculative than computational functionalism. — Seth (2026), p. 15

His essay for Noema the same year rests the case for life on it:

I should say upfront that I don’t have a knock-down argument for this position, nor do I think any such argument yet exists. But it is worth taking seriously, if only for the simple reason mentioned earlier: every candidate for consciousness that most people currently agree on as actually being conscious is also alive. — Seth, “The Mythology Of Conscious AI”, Noema (2026)

In his response to the commentators it is the fourth of four reasons to treat his view as the default:

… and (iv) the only systems we are presently sure are conscious are living systems – setting an empirical prior. — Seth (2026), Response, p. 105

Ned Block takes the step from us to everything in so many words:

There is little doubt that some aspect of our biology is necessary for consciousness in us, and if necessary in us, might be necessary, period. — Block, “Can only meat machines be conscious?” (2026), p. 307

Both are careful. Seth says “suggests” and “prior”, and Block says “might”. The question is whether the observation supports even that much.

Rebuke 1: every known example has everything in common

Every conscious system we know of is alive. Every one is also made of carbon, runs on water, has DNA, has neurons, evolved on Earth, will die and had a mother. A sample from one lineage shares everything the lineage shares. It cannot say which of those features matter.

Seth agrees for carbon:

Neither do I endorse carbon chauvinism (the claim that only carbon-based life can be conscious). — Seth (2026), p. 10

The observation supports “needs carbon” exactly as well as it supports “needs life”. Seth rejects one and keeps the other for reasons from his theory of predictive processing and the free energy principle. Then the theory is doing the work, and “all known examples” adds nothing to it.

Leonard Dung put the cost in his commentary. A requirement of life rules out more than computers:

It also rules out consciousness in creatures, for example, hypothetical extraterrestrials, which are not made from silicon but which also do not qualify as living creatures. — Dung (2026), commentary on Seth

Rebuke 2: it has been run before

Before 1828 every known organic compound came from a living thing. Chemists concluded that only life could make them. Ira Remsen described the inference in 1875:

These were known to occur only in the organs of plants or animals. The two facts, taken together, were significant, and but little surprise can be expressed that a connection was traced between them. The simplest conclusion that could be drawn from the premises was drawn … Chemical substances which are produced under the mysterious influence of life, in the dark, unfathomed cavities of living organisms, cannot be produced by the hand of the mortal chemist. — Remsen, Popular Science Monthly (1875)

Then Friedrich Wöhler made urea from a cyanate. The dogma did not fall. It retreated: the cyanate had come from animals. When that failed it retreated again, to “the complicated form of union found in the greater number of the products of the life-process.” Remsen’s verdict: “The old dogma no longer exists.”

In 1903 every known flier was an animal. Simon Newcomb, the leading American astronomer, drew the conclusion weeks before the Wright brothers flew:

We may look on the bird as a sort of flying-machine complete in itself, of which a brain and nervous system are fundamentally necessary parts. No such machine can navigate the air unless guided by something having life. — Newcomb, “The Outlook for the Flying-Machine” (1903)

Newcomb also gave an argument from weight, so he was not relying on the induction alone. But he did rely on it: the bird used “a kind of force which is peculiar to the animal creation”.

Kenneth Oakley’s Man the Tool-maker was more careful. Shaping a tool for a future use, he wrote, “is beyond the mental capacity of any known apes.” “Any known apes” is the argument in three words. In 1960 Jane Goodall watched chimpanzees strip twigs to fish for termites. As the Jane Goodall Institute tells it, Louis Leakey telegraphed: “Now we must redefine tool, redefine man or accept chimpanzees as human.”

Two commentators on Seth made the flight comparison. Ron Chrisley took Seth’s own sentences about consciousness, put flight in their place, and asked whether artificial flight is therefore impossible: “Obviously not.”

Seth’s response

Moreover, while flight has a consensus functional description – broadly, anything that moves through the air in a controlled and sustained way – consciousness does not. — Seth (2026), Response, p. 95

That is true, and it is a reason why the examples cannot settle the matter. It is not a reason why they settle it in favour of life. If we do not know what consciousness is, we do not know which features of the known cases it depends on. Block said so in 2002: a fundamentally different physical realisation “is not a ground of rational belief in lack of consciousness.”

Rebuke 3: Seth’s own objection

Matthias Michel’s commentary ran an induction the other way. Memory, language and arithmetic have all been explained functionally, so consciousness probably will be too. Seth’s reply:

Michel’s argument is a variety of induction, but the key inductive step is unsupported. … Assuming that computationalist explanations will work as well for the latter as for the former requires assuming that consciousness is relevantly similar – i.e., that computations are sufficient – which is precisely the claim at issue. — Seth (2026), Response, p. 93

That is a fair objection, and it applies to reason (iv). Projecting from the living examples to all conscious systems requires assuming that being alive is the relevant similarity. That is precisely the claim at issue.

Rebuke 4: the sample was chosen by resemblance to us

“The only systems we are presently sure are conscious” is not a random sample of conscious systems. It is the set we are sure of, and we are sure of it by comparison with ourselves. Block states the method:

The perspective I will develop is that when deciding whether a non-human system is conscious, we have no choice but to extrapolate from ourselves. — Block (2026), p. 298

A sample selected by likeness to us will be like us, and we are alive. The observation that every member is alive records how the sample was chosen. It would be true whether or not life matters to consciousness.

The same method has a record, and Seth gives it in his conclusion: “our unfortunate track record of withholding moral status from conscious things – from many non-human animals, and sometimes even from other humans.” Each entry on that list is a conscious being left out of the sample. Seth goes on:

Will withholding attributions of consciousness to AI leave us once more on the wrong side of history? I believe the situation here is different. Our psychological biases are more likely to lead to false positives than false negatives. — Seth (2026), p. 15

The record he has just described is of false negatives. No argument is given for why the next case will be different.

Jeremy Pober and Eric Schwitzgebel put the larger point as a principle of mediocrity. If sophisticated agents arise many times in the universe, in many substrates, it would be odd if only those sharing our chemistry were conscious:

Absent some reason to think we are special, we Earthlings would then be suspiciously, inexplicably lucky. — Pober and Schwitzgebel (2026), p. 15

Their response

Seth’s fallback is that his view asks for less:

CF is a claim about sufficiency across substrates. This imposes a high evidential burden which I believe remains unmet … BN is the logically lighter claim that some biological substrate properties are necessary for consciousness — Seth (2026), Response, p. 93

A claim of necessity is not lighter. “Nothing that is not alive is conscious” is a statement about every system there is or could be, on this planet or any other. It carries the same burden as the claim it opposes. The default, while nobody can meet either burden, is to say that we do not know. Colin Allen’s commentary says this in one sentence: “No one is in a position to say what really matters for consciousness, or whether only living systems can instantiate the complexity needed for conscious feelings.”

Scope of the rebukes

None of this shows that any AI system is conscious, or that biology is irrelevant. Life may be necessary. Seth’s positive arguments from predictive processing and metabolism are separate from the induction and are not assessed here. Chalmers, who rejects the biological view, reports that philosophers give it a collective credence of about one in three.

Nor does the Copernican argument carry over to AI without qualification. Pober and Schwitzgebel argue elsewhere that systems built to mimic us are a different case from aliens that evolved on their own, because mimicry can reproduce the signs of consciousness without the thing. That objection is about mimicry, not about life.

The rebuke is to one sentence: that the list of conscious things we already recognise tells us what consciousness requires.

In their favour

Seth’s warning about over-attribution is right. Fluent text exploits the habit of seeing minds, and he is right that models “confabulate” rather than “hallucinate”. He does not claim that conscious AI is impossible, only that it is unlikely on present lines. He says he has no knock-down argument, which is honest.

And the induction is not worthless inside its range. Within one lineage shared ancestry is good evidence: a mammal’s pain is probably like ours because its nervous system is inherited from the same ancestors. The mistake is to carry the inference outside the lineage that licenses it.
