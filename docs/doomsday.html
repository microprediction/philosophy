Doomsday and Simulation

Assume you are a random sample of all the humans who will ever live, and doom is near. Assume you are a random sample of all minds like yours, and you are probably software. Two conclusions from one move.

Defendants
Brandon Carter and John Leslie (the Doomsday argument; Leslie, The End of the World, 1996); J. Richard Gott III, Nature (1993); Nick Bostrom, “Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?” (2003); and Elon Musk (2016) for the popular version
Claim
Treat yourself as a random draw from all observers. Then humanity probably ends soon (Doomsday), or at least one of three surprising things is true, one of which is that we live in a simulation (Bostrom). Musk: “one in billions” that we are not simulated.
Charge
Turning a fact about averages over a population into a probability about one known member, after choosing the population to suit.
Verdict
Gott: guilty. His rule ignores what we know, and it fails wherever there is something to know. Carter and Leslie: not proven. The argument stands or falls on a sampling assumption that a rival assumption exactly cancels. Bostrom: acquitted on the trilemma, which is careful and mostly arithmetic. Musk: guilty. He deleted two of Bostrom’s three options.

The argument, in their words

The Doomsday argument has two versions. Gott’s uses time. He reasons that if nothing is special about the moment you look at something, that moment is uniformly distributed over its lifetime. So with 95% probability you are not in the first or last 2.5%, and the future duration lies between 1/39 and 39 times the past duration. The abstract of his 1993 Nature paper:

Making only the assumption that you are a random intelligent observer, limits for the total longevity of our species of 0.2 million to 8 million years can be derived at the 95% confidence level. — Gott, Nature 363 (1993), abstract

Homo sapiens is about 200,000 years old, so the future is between 5,100 and 7.8 million years. Gott has applied the same rule to the Berlin Wall, Stonehenge, Broadway plays, Nature itself, the Chicago White Sox and himself.

Carter and Leslie use birth rank. Carter thought of it first and did not publish. Leslie put it in print from 1989 on, and in a book, The End of the World, in 1996. In the illustration Bostrom and Olum use, about 60 billion people have been born so far. If humanity ends soon, with 200 billion in all, your rank is unremarkable. If it lasts and 200 trillion are born, you are among the first 0.03%. Being that early is a thousand times less likely, so update toward doom. Unlike Gott, Leslie starts from a prior and applies Bayes’ rule, and he says the argument only shifts probability. Bostrom gave the assumption a name:

(SSA) One should reason as if one were a random sample from the set of all observers in one’s reference class. — Bostrom, Anthropic Bias (2002), ch. 3

Bostrom’s simulation paper uses a weaker cousin of this to reach a disjunction:

This paper argues that at least one of the following propositions is true: (1) the human species is very likely to go extinct before reaching a “posthuman” stage; (2) any posthuman civilization is extremely unlikely to run a significant number of simulations of their evolutionary history (or variations thereof); (3) we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation. — Bostrom (2003), abstract

At Recode’s Code Conference in 2016, Elon Musk gave the version most people have heard. Games went from Pong to photorealism in forty years, so they will become indistinguishable from reality, and there will be billions of machines running them. Asked what he thought:

There’s a one in billions chance we’re in base reality. — Musk (2016), as transcribed by Ezra Klein, Vox

Rebuke 1: the rule is right on average and wrong for nearly everyone

Gott’s rule gives every phenomenon of age t a 50% chance of reaching age 2t, whatever the phenomenon is. Mark Brown sent Andrew Gelman the obvious test:

Apply the method to a 90 year old man and the 50% prediction confidence interval for his residual life will be (30,270). Apply it to his 9 year old great grandson and you get (3,27). — Mark Brown, quoted by Gelman (2018)

Gelman’s reply:

Hey, living to the age of 270—that’d be cool! I just have to make it to 90, then I’m most of the way there. — Gelman (2018)

There is a precise sense in which Gott is right, and it is worth seeing. Take a population with stable births and a realistic mortality curve, and pick a living person at random without looking at their age. Their age is then uniformly placed within their lifetime, and Gott’s intervals have exactly the coverage he claims. I checked this with a Gompertz–Makeham mortality model (hazard 0.0005 + 0.00003 e0.095 age, life expectancy 77 years; the script is in the notes). Averaged over everyone alive, the 50% intervals cover 50.0% of the time and the 95% intervals cover 94.9%. Now condition on the age, which is the only thing Gott uses:

The 50% interval is right for almost nobody in particular. It averages to 50% because it is nearly always right for the middle-aged and nearly always wrong for the old and the young. The model is illustrative, but no plausible mortality curve changes the pattern. Gelman put the statistical point in 2005:

If you look carefully at classical statistical theory, you’ll see that it makes claims about averages, not about particular cases. However, this does not mean that there is a 95% chance that any particular interval will contain the true value. Especially not in this situation, where we have additional subject-matter knowledge. — Gelman (2005)

His summary: “The doomsday argument is pretty silly (and also, it’s not Bayesian).” Carlton Caves reached the same place by a different route. Gott’s rule is the Bayesian answer under one particular prior, proportional to 1/T² in total duration, which is the prior that has no time scale in it. Caves’s abstract:

Nothing licenses indiscriminate use of Gott’s rule as a predictor of future duration. It should only be used when the phenomenon in question has no identifiable time scales. — Caves (2008), abstract

Humans have a time scale. So do dogs, baseball careers and species.

Rebuke 2: Caves offered to bet

In 1999 Caves collected the names, breeds and birth dates of 24 dogs owned by people in his department and lodged a notarised list. For each of the six dogs over ten years old, Gott’s rule says a 50% chance of living to twice its age.

To sweeten the pot, I offered Gott 2:1 odds in his favor. Gott refused the bets on the grounds that “I don’t do bets.” If he had believed his own predictions, his expected gain would have been $3,000 US, and the probability that he would have been a net loser on the six bets was 7/64=0.11. I contacted the caretakers during May and June of 2008 and verified that all six dogs have died. Thus, as I fully expected, I would have won all the bets and been $6,000 US richer. — Caves (2008), sec. 4

Under Gott’s rule the chance that all six dogs fail to reach twice their age is 1/64. Caves would have put the chance near 1, because a dog of eleven is not going to see twenty-two.

Caves did the same arithmetic for baseball. In June 2008 the Chicago Cubs had not won the World Series since 1908. Gott’s rule gives a 50% chance that the drought lasts another 99 years. Giving every one of 30 clubs an equal chance each year gives (29/30)99 = 3.5%. The Cubs won in 2016. Gott’s rule had given that about an 8% chance (8.4 more years on a 100-year drought); the equal-chance model gave it about 26%. Caves had done the White Sox too: in 1996 Gott’s rule gave a title by 2005 a 10% chance against 26% for equal chances, and they won in 2005.

Gott’s response

According to Caves, Gott replied that his rule applies to a dog drawn at random from the sample, not to particular dogs whose ages are known. Caves agrees, and that is the point:

It is obvious that in a large sample of dogs selected without regard to age, roughly half the dogs, within the inevitable statistical fluctuations, will be in the first half of their lives, with the rest in the second half. This is the trivial content of Gott’s Copernican principle. It is equally obvious that having a sample in which half the dogs are in the first half of their lives does not imply that any particular dog in the sample has a probability of 1/2 to survive beyond twice its present age. — Caves (2008), sec. 4

Humanity is one particular dog, of known age. Gott’s prediction for it is made on exactly the basis he declined to bet on for the dogs.

Rebuke 3: the answer depends on who “we” are

“A random intelligent observer” has to be random within some class. The class is not given by nature, and Gott’s own arithmetic moves with it. Here is his 95% rule applied to some reasonable choices of what we are, measured in years:

Reference classAge so far95% interval for the future
Genus Homoabout 2 million years51,000 to 78 million years
Homo sapiens (Gott’s choice)about 200,000 years5,100 to 7.8 million years
Farming societiesabout 10,000 years260 to 390,000 years
People who have read the Doomsday argument in print37 years (Leslie, 1989)about 1 to 1,440 years

Each row is equally Copernican. The last row is the class that is actually doing the reasoning, and it predicts the end of Doomsday reasoners, not of people. The birth-rank version has the same problem: count all humans, or only those who could follow the argument, or only those alive after some date, and the likelihood ratio changes. Radford Neal, commenting on Gelman’s 2005 post:

One problem with the Doomsday Argument is that there isn’t just one of them – there are several, some Bayesian, some not, propounded with varying degrees of statistical literacy. None of them are precise (due to the “reference class” problem), which is one argument for why they’re invalid. — Neal, comment on Gelman (2005)

Bostrom, who is sympathetic to the argument and wrote the book on it, agrees about both versions. On Gott: “Gott’s version is incorrect.” On Leslie:

We found shortcomings in the versions that have been presented in the literature, we argued that John Leslie’s proposal for solving the reference class problem is unworkable, and we showed that DA has alternative interpretations and is inconclusive. — Bostrom, Anthropic Bias (2002), ch. 10

Rebuke 4: a rival assumption cancels it exactly

The Carter–Leslie update uses one likelihood: if N people will ever live, the chance of having any particular birth rank is 1/N. A world with more people makes your rank less probable. But a world with more people also makes it more probable that you exist to have a rank at all. Dennis Dieks raised this in 1992, and Bostrom calls it the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). Weight each world by its number of observers and the two factors of N cancel. Ken Olum:

Under this treatment, it is more likely to exist at all in a race which is long-lived, as originally discussed by Dieks, and this cancels the Doomsday Argument, so that the chance of a disaster is only what one would ordinarily estimate. — Olum (2002), abstract

With the same numbers and even prior odds between 200 billion and 200 trillion people, the self-sampling assumption gives doom-soon a probability of 1/(1 + 0.001) = 99.9%. Add the self-indication weight and it returns to 50%, which is where you started. The argument adds nothing, and the forecast is whatever your study of the actual hazards says it is. Olum’s point about those hazards:

It doesn’t really matter how long it took and how many people lived and died before we achieved the technology to destroy ourselves. — Olum (2002), sec. IV

Bostrom’s response

SIA has its own absurd consequence. Bostrom’s Presumptuous Philosopher tells physicists choosing between two cosmologies, one with a trillion times more observers, that they need not run the experiment:

“Hey guys, it is completely unnecessary for you to do the experiment, because I can already show to you that T2 is about a trillion times more likely to be true than T1!” — Bostrom, Anthropic Bias (2002), ch. 7

That is a fair hit, and I do not think SIA is obviously right. But it does not rescue Doomsday. It shows that two candidate rules for reasoning about your own existence each give a strange answer somewhere, and that the Doomsday conclusion is an output of the choice. An argument whose conclusion vanishes under a rival assumption that nobody has ruled out has not established its conclusion. Bostrom’s own eventual position is that neither assumption is needed in its simple form, and that the Doomsday argument is inconclusive.

Rebuke 5: Bostrom is not Musk

Bostrom’s argument is close to arithmetic. Let fP be the fraction of human-level civilisations that reach a posthuman stage, fI the fraction of those interested in running ancestor simulations, and NI the number such a civilisation runs, each roughly as populous as the real history. The fraction of human-type observers who are simulated is

fsim = fPfINI / (fPfINI + 1).

If NI is enormous, either fPfI is tiny or fsim is close to 1. That is the trilemma, and given substrate-independence and the premise about computing power, it is valid. Bostrom then declines to say which horn is true:

In the dark forest of our current ignorance, it seems sensible to apportion one’s credence roughly evenly between (1), (2), and (3). — Bostrom (2003), sec. VII

That puts his credence that we are simulated at something like one in three. Musk’s “one in billions” for base reality needs fPfINI of about a billion. In other words it needs the first two horns to be false with near certainty, which is to assume that civilisations like ours survive and want to run simulations. Musk’s premise was that games keep improving. Bostrom’s first horn, extinction before maturity, is the Doomsday question again, and nobody knows its answer. Bostrom’s FAQ describes the move:

I note that people who hear about the simulation argument often react by saying, “Yes, I accept the argument, and it is obvious that it is alternative n that obtains.” But different people pick a different n. Some think it obvious that (1) is true, others that (2) is true, yet others that (3) is true. Many if not all of these people are overconfident. — Bostrom, The Simulation Argument FAQ (2025), Q2

The FAQ also has an entry titled “What if one is Elon Musk?”, which begins: “This is not actually a frequently asked question, but perhaps it should be.”

Bostrom also saw the connection this page is about, and argued that his principle is weaker than Doomsday’s:

The Doomsday argument rests on a much stronger and more controversial premiss, namely that one should reason as if one were a random sample from the set of all people who will ever have lived (past, present, and future) even though we know that we are living in the early twenty‑first century rather than at some point in the distant past or the future. The bland indifference principle, by contrast, applies only to cases where we have no information about which group of people we belong to. — Bostrom (2003), sec. V

That is a real distinction, and it is the right one. Gott and Leslie know their position in the sequence and throw the knowledge away. Bostrom’s principle applies only when nothing you can observe tells simulated and unsimulated minds apart.

Rebuke 6: what the simulation argument still borrows

The weak point is the step from “most observers like us are simulated” to “I probably am”. It needs the class “observers with human-type experiences” to be the right one, and it needs us to have no information that distinguishes our case. Sean Carroll doubted both:

If we know that we are members of some very homogeneous ensemble, where every member is basically the same, then by all means typicality is a sensible assumption. But when ensembles are highly heterogeneous, and we actually know something about our specific situation, there’s no reason to assume we are typical. As James Hartle and Mark Srednicki have pointed out, that’s a fake kind of humility — by asserting that “we are typical” in the multiverse, we’re actually claiming that “typical observers are like us.” — Carroll (2016)

Carroll added a puzzle. Simulated civilisations can run simulations of their own, with less computing power at each level, so the hierarchy bottoms out in worlds too crude to simulate anything. If observers are counted, most of them live at the bottom. So the typical observer cannot build the simulations the argument started from, and we are building increasingly good ones. Carroll argued against “one version” of the argument, not Bostrom’s exact text, but Bostrom’s third horn needs the same counting.

The physicists’ complaint is about explanation rather than probability. Sabine Hossenfelder:

“The programmer did it” isn’t science. It’s not even pseudoscience. It’s just words. — Hossenfelder (2017)

Scott Aaronson agreed that the hypothesis is lazy, “that it doesn’t pay its rent by doing real explanatory work”, while rejecting Hossenfelder’s claim that known physics nearly rules it out.

Bostrom’s response

Bostrom answers the unfalsifiability charge in his FAQ, and the answer is a forecaster’s answer. Evidence about one horn moves the others. If we found a hazard that destroys nearly every civilisation before maturity, (1) would rise and (3) would fall. If we approach technological maturity “with no clear peril in sight”, (1) falls and (2) and (3) rise. That is right. It makes the simulation hypothesis a quantity that can be moved by evidence, even if no experiment settles it. It also means the probability of (3) is currently whatever we think about extinction and about the tastes of posthumans, which is to say not much.

Scope

None of this says humanity is safe. Extinction risk is real, and Leslie gave much attention to actual hazards; Bostrom lists nuclear war, meteors, particle-physics experiments and nanotechnology among them. Those deserve estimates. The rebuke is to the idea that our birth rank or our species’ age supplies one for free.

Nor does it say we are not simulated. Bostrom’s disjunction may well be true, and I cannot say which horn holds. The rebuke to the simulation side is narrow: to the step from “at least one of three” to “almost certainly the third”.

And the dispute between SSA and SIA is not settled here. The claim is only that an argument which depends on an unsettled choice between them is not a result about the future.

In their favour

Gott’s first prediction came true. He visited the Berlin Wall in 1969, when it was eight years old, and told a friend there was a 50% chance it would last between 2⅔ and 24 more years. It came down twenty years later. On 27 May 1993, the day the Nature paper appeared, he gave 95% intervals for the 44 Broadway and off-Broadway shows then running, and he reports that they did well. Olum checked Broadway runs from 1970 to 1990 and found why: for shows past their first few months, the share running a given length falls off roughly as T−1.7, close to the 1/T² that makes Gott’s rule exact. For long-running shows the rule had the right prior by luck or instinct. Olum also found it fails for new shows: a show open 44 days gets a 97.5% chance of closing within 156 days from Gott, against 78% in the data.

That is the honest case for the rule. Where a phenomenon has no time scale you know of, the scale-free answer is sensible, and Caves says so. It resembles the Lindy heuristic that old books and old firms will last longer than new ones, which is often a good bet. The error is to use it where you do know something.

Leslie used priors and Bayes’ rule, said plainly that the argument only shifts probabilities, and used it to argue for taking extinction risk seriously at a time when few did. Carter, Leslie and Bostrom turned a vague worry about observation selection into explicit assumptions that could be argued over, which is how the cancellation in Rebuke 4 was found.

Bostrom is more careful than his reputation. His paper concludes with a disjunction, says how it differs from Doomsday, refuses to give a number, and calls the people who are sure of one horn overconfident. The simulation argument is better than the popular version it produced.
